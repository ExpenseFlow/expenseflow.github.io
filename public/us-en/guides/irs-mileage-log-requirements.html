How to keep an IRS-compliant mileage log

If you deduct vehicle use for business, the IRS expects records that back up every mile. Here is what a defensible mileage log contains and how to keep one without the paperwork.

Home › Guides › IRS mileage log requirements

What the IRS expects you to record

To claim business use of a vehicle, you need adequate records or sufficient evidence supporting each trip. For every business trip, a good log captures:

  • Date of the trip
  • Destination (and starting point)
  • Business purpose: who you met or what you did
  • Miles driven for the trip

You should also record your total miles for the year (an odometer reading at the start and end of the year is the simplest way) so your business use can be shown as a share of total driving.

Make the record at the time of the trip

Records made at or near the time of the trip carry more weight than a spreadsheet rebuilt in March. This is the main reason automatic GPS tracking is easier to defend: the data is timestamped as you drive.

Standard mileage rate or actual expenses?

You generally choose between two methods. The standard mileage rate multiplies your business miles by a rate the IRS sets each year. Actual expenses deduct the business share of gas, repairs, insurance and depreciation, which means you also need receipts. Either way, the mileage log is what establishes your business percentage. What does not count

  • Commuting between home and a regular workplace is generally not deductible.
  • Personal errands mixed into a business trip should be separated out.
  • Trips with no recorded purpose are the first to be challenged.

Common mistakes

  • Estimating miles from memory at year end
  • Missing the business purpose on each trip
  • Not keeping total annual miles
  • Losing the log: keep records for as long as they may be needed for your return

Frequently asked questions

Does the IRS require a mileage log?

If you claim a vehicle deduction, you need adequate records or sufficient evidence for each business trip. A log with date, destination, purpose and miles is the standard way to show it.

Can I use an app instead of a paper log?

Yes. The IRS cares about the content and timing of the record, not the format. An app that records trips as they happen and lets you add a business purpose can meet the requirement.

Is commuting deductible?

Generally no. Travel between your home and a regular place of work is treated as commuting. Trips between work locations or to clients can qualify.
